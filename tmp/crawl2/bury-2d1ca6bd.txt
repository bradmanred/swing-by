Main content area</h2>
Giant Hogweed, Japanese Knotweed and Himalayan Balsam are non-native plants and are highly invasive as they have no natural enemies to control them in the United Kingdom. As a result, they out compete native plants leading to large groups of plants that grow very tall.</p>
This page provides guidance for you to:</p>
Find out how to identify these plants</li>
Know what to do if you find them on private or public land</li>
 </div></div>
Giant Hogweed</h2>
Giant Hogweed is a fast growing invasive plant that mainly grows next to water, in damp meadows or on derelict land.</p></div></div>
Giant Hogweed sap can cause painful burns to humans and animals, making skin sensitive to strong sunlight.</p>
 Do not touch these plants. If you do come into contact with the sap, you should wash the area thoroughly, keep out of direct sunlight and seek medical attention.</p></div></div></div>
 Giant Hogweed plants. </div>
Identifying Giant Hogweed</h2>
Giant Hogweed is a tall, cow parsley-like plant with thick bristly stems that are often purple-blotched. It can reach a height of 13 feet (4 meters).The flowers are white and held in umbels facing upwards about 50 centimetres in diameter, looking like flat-topped clusters similar to carrots or cow parsley.</p></div></div>
Giant Hogweed on private land</h2>
If you see Giant Hogweed on private land, such as a neighbouring property, construction site or agricultural fields, please inform the land owner. It is not illegal to have Giant Hogweed growing on your land, but it is an offence to cause or allow it to spread. Control of this plant is the responsibility of the landowner. You must not:</p>
Remove or dispose of Giant Hogweed as it could cause the plant to spread, which is an offence</li>
Dispose of Giant Hogweed in your brown recycling bin or take it to a household waste and recycling centre, as this is an offence. It must be disposed of as controlled waste</li>
Giant Hogweed on public land</h2>
We make every effort to contain the spread of Giant Hogweed and Japanese Knotweed on public land. If you do find any on public land within the borough of Bury, please report this to us.</p></div></div>
 Report Giant Hogweed on public land </div></div>
Japanese Knotweed</h2>
Japanese Knotweed is a highly invasive weed which overwhelms other plants. It spreads rapidly, up to 10 cm per day, and can reach a height of 10 feet (3 meters). It can:</p>
Grow almost anywhere</li>
Cause structural damage to buildings and other structures by growing through cracks in masonry</li>
Reduce land values</li>
Cause difficulty in getting a mortgage</li>
 </div></div>
 Japanese Knotweed plant. </div>
Identifying Japanese Knotweed</h2>
Japanese Knotweed has green shovel shaped leaves, bamboo-like stems and small white flowers during the months of September and October.</p></div></div>
Japanese Knotweed on private land</h2>
It is not illegal to have Japanese Knotweed growing on your land, but it is an offence to cause or allow it to spread. Control of the plants is the responsibility of the landowner. You must not:</p>
Remove or dispose of Japanese Knotweed as it could cause the plant to spread, which is an offence</li>
Dispose of Japanese Knotweed in your brown recycling bin or take it to a household waste and recycling centre, as this is an offence. It must be disposed of as controlled waste</li>
If you see Japanese Knotweed on private land, such as a neighbouring property, construction site or agricultural fields, please inform the land owner.</p>
Japanese Knotweed on public land</h2>
We make every effort to contain the spread of Giant Hogweed and Japanese Knotweed on public land. If you do find any on public land within the borough of Bury, please report this to us.</p></div></div>
 Report Japanese Knotweed on public land </div></div>
Himalayan Balsam</h2>
Himalayan balsam was introduced as a garden plant in 1839, but soon escaped and became widely naturalised along riverbanks and ditches, especially close to towns.</p>
It is a non-native invasive plant species that out-competes native plant species in ecologically sensitive areas, particularly riverbanks.</p>
It's dense stands can increase the likelihood of flooding during high rainfall and, during winter, die back leaving the riverbanks exposed to erosion.</p>
Its explosive seed pods aid its spread by sending the seeds into the river, causing further dispersal downstream. It flowers from July to October.</p>
 It is an offence to plant or otherwise allow this species to grow in the wild. </p></div></div>
 Himalayan Balsam plant. </div>
How you can help control Himalayan Balsam</h2></div>
 Bury Community Himalayan Balsam Control Strategy<!-- --> 
</path> </h3>
To combat the growth of this plant successfully we have put together the 'Bury Community Himalayan Balsam Strategy', by following this plan you can help stop the spread:</p>
 Wear gardening gloves: As a safety precaution it is always recommended that you wear suitable gear that you don’t mind getting mucky</li>
 Uproot: Once you have identified the balsam, grab the stem from its lowest point, giving it a sturdy tug to uproot the whole plant</li>
 Break or damage the root: Using your hands or foot, snap the root off the plant just above its lowest ‘nobble’ point. Doing this will ensure it doesn’t re-root itself</li>
 Pile Up: With each plant you pull up, start to build a compost pile so that the process of natural degradation can begin. If you are near a public footpath make sure that the pile isn’t blocking access for passers-by</li>
 Repeat and revisit: It’s always good to revisit sites to make sure there is no re-growth</li>
 Please note: Do not pull it once it has seed heads as this will help to spread the seed. </p></div></div></div></div></div></div>
Identifying Himalayan Balsam</h2>
Himalayan Balsam has large, pink flowers shaped like a bonnet; these are followed by hanging, green seed pods.</p></div>

Other non-native plants</h2>
The Non-Native Species Secretariat (NNSS) has responsibility for helping to co-ordinate the approach to invasive non-native species in Great Britain.</p>
If you are interested in finding our more about other invasive non-native species, you can visit the NNSS website to look up information about specific species and learn how to identify them.</p></div></div></div></div></div>
