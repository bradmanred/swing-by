 History of Heanor Memorial Park      Heanor Memorial Park has long been a central green space for the local community, offering both recreation and remembrance. The park was established in the early 20th century as a tribute to the men of Heanor who lost their lives during the First World War. Like many “memorial parks” created across Britain after the war, it was designed not only as a place of reflection, but also as a living space for future generations to enjoy.     The land was carefully laid out with formal gardens, open lawns, and tree-lined paths, reflecting the style of civic parks of that era. Over time, additional features such as play areas, sports facilities, and ornamental planting were introduced, ensuring the park remained relevant to changing community needs.     At the heart of the park stands the war memorial, which continues to serve as a focal point for remembrance events, particularly on Remembrance Sunday. These ceremonies honour those who served in both World Wars and subsequent conflicts, reinforcing the park’s role as a place of collective memory.     Throughout the decades, Heanor Memorial Park has evolved while retaining its original purpose—a space where heritage, community, and leisure come together. Today, it remains one of Heanor’s most valued public spaces, welcoming residents and visitors alike.

 Fallen Sculpture   
 </h1>
</div>
Fallen, a sculpture by local artist Andy Mayers is a tribute  </div>
to the people of Heanor in recognition of their sacrifice and dedication to the war efforts during WWII. The sculpture was commissioned by Heanor & Loscoe Town Council and features a father in uniform kissing his child goodbye, against a backdrop of leaves falling from a tree. The falling leaves symbolise the 137 soldiers from Heanor and surrounding districts who gave their lives during World War Two.  </div>

 The Official Opening  
 </h2>
 </div>
At the conclusion of the Second World War local peoples thoughts turned towards providing a fitting memorial for those men killed in the hostilities. At a public meeting held at the Town Hall on 31 October 1945, it was decided to provide a public park. </div>
 </div>
The aim was that “all people, young and old, could enjoy the beauties of nature in lovely surroundings, near to the centre of the town.” The centrepiece was a traditional war memorial. </div>
 </div>
The main gates, opposite Mundy Street, came from Derby Lodge entrance to Shipley Hall, and were donated by Shipley Collieries Ltd. The second set of gates opposite the Miners’ Welfare came from the gardens of Shipley Hall. </div></div>
 </div>
 </div>
The Park was officially opened on 28 April 1951 by the Duke of Devonshire, and a dedication service was led by the Archdeacon of Chesterfield, assisted by a cross-denomination array of local church ministers. </div>
 </div>
The Park was formally handed over by the Chairman of the Memorial Park Committee to the Chairman of the District Council, Councillor William Belfield. Ownership of the Park transferred to Amber Valley Borough Council in 1974. </div>
 </div>
In the 1980’s, the Second World War Memorial was supplemented by a small additional memorial plaque commemorating those who died in the Falklands Conflict of 1982. </div></div>
